Stehende Licthwellen und die Schwingungsrichtung polarisirten Lichtes; Von otto Wiener

Standing Light Waves and the Direction of Oscillation of Polarized Light; By Otto Wiener


In his investigation of electrodynamic waves in
air space, Hertz simply determined the phase shift
of the electric oscillations caused by reflection
by causing the incident and reflected
waves to interfere, thereby subjecting the phenomenon
of standing electric waves to experimental examination.
The corresponding question in optics—concerning the phase
change of light upon perpendicular reflection—had, until
now, still not found a definitive answer.
Since this had already occupied my thoughts earlier,
Hertz’s investigation inspired me to attempt, by the same
method—that is, also with the aid of standing waves—to
resolve the controversial issue in optics.
However, standing light waves had not yet been made
accessible to experimentation. So I thought about
whether this might be made possible in some way.
Just as Hertz had used a current path with an activated spark gap
to investigate standing electrodynamic
waves,
and just as Kundt had used a lightly moving powder
to measure standing air waves,Thus, for the light waves described by Kachweis,
the only thing that mattered was finding a suitable indication
that light was moving
at a given location.
Of the methods known to us for objectively
detecting the movement of light—such as through thermal effects, fluorescence,
breath images, and chemical-photographic effects—
the latter seemed to me to be the most suitable.
The standard gelatin plates, however, were
simply not suitable for
this purpose. For, apart
from their opacity, the thickness of such
a plate is measured in hundredths of a millimeter (I), whereas
the distance between two consecutive nodes
of a standing light wave is calculated in ten-thousandths
of a millimeter.
Accordingly, about one hundred wave patterns
could fit along the thickness of a gelatin plate.
If, therefore, the plate were exposed to the effect of a standing
light wave and examined after development,
one would see, at every point on the plate, the superimposed effects of
100 wave patterns; the plate would
appear to be uniformly blackened.
An investigation
of the standing wave is conceivable only if
its effect can be observed separately over a distance that is a small
fraction of the wavelength.
The task at hand was therefore to determine whether it was possible
to produce a transparent, light-sensitive layer whose
thickness y is sufficiently small relative to the wavelength of light.
While studying the relevant photographic
literature, I came across references—as early as the daguerreotype—
that suggested the idea that stationary
light waves may have played a role, albeit unrecognized,
in this earliest form of photography.
As is well known, a daguerreotype is produced by a weak,
superficial iodination of a silver plate. The resulting
coloration of the plate is, according to the authors’
statements, decisive for its light sensitivity. 2,
This coloration arises, as is well known, through interference and
depends on the thickness of the silver iodide layer.
